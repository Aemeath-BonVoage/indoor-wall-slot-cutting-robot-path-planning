# Indoor Wall Slot-Cutting Robot: Fine Alignment, Code-Constrained Slot Planning and Multi-Station Execution

**Abstract of the technical report** (full report in Chinese: [README.md](README.md))

**Problem.** Electrical conduits in residential walls are embedded in slots cut into the wall. A mobile manipulator that cuts them must position its base to millimetre accuracy: the arm reaches only about 12 cm along the wall, and every base pose error is amplified at the tool tip by a 0.69 m lever arm. It must also plan slot routes that satisfy building codes while minimising execution time, hold its position against the cutting force, and it must be evaluated in a simulator that actually behaves as commanded.

**System.** The robot is simulated in ROS 2 Humble and Gazebo Classic. It has a mecanum omnidirectional base, a decoupled Cartesian 4-DoF arm, a 2D LiDAR and a front camera. The pipeline runs end to end. SLAM Toolbox and Nav2 bring the base to the wall, and fine alignment parks it 0.70 m in front of a fiducial marker. An offline planner outputs slot routes, base stations and arm waypoints. A behaviour tree (BehaviorTree.CPP, structured like the Nav2 `bt_navigator`) then sequences skill actions that visit the stations, hold the base, compensate residual pose errors and cut, with the arm waypoints of each station executed as one continuous trajectory. Nav2 uses Smac 2D planning and an omnidirectional MPPI controller. The base runs under ros2_control with a custom mecanum controller and a simulated hardware interface. Its wheel odometry includes an encoder error model and is fused with a simulated gyro by a `robot_localization` EKF. Cutting is simulated with a 10 mm tool-to-wall gap, since Gazebo cannot model material removal; an optional plugin applies a modelled cutting wrench to the base. Gazebo ground truth is used only for evaluation.

**Time convention.** Unless stated otherwise, times are *station time*: the sum over stations of strafing, settling, residual measurement and cutting, excluding navigation and the first alignment. *Total task time* includes navigation, alignment and re-alignment.

**Results.** Numbers are Gazebo measurements unless marked *offline*.

- **Fine alignment.** Detection, estimation, control and the arrival test are separated, following Nav2 `opennav_docking`. The detector uses AprilTag with edge-fitted sub-pixel corners and IPPE. A wall-relative EKF over (distance, lateral offset, heading) uses exact SE(2) odometry increments, latency compensation and χ² gating. The control law is a square-root-shaped saturated proportional law, globally asymptotically stable for the integrator plant (V = ½|e|²). A discrete-time pole analysis with sampling, velocity-loop lag and one-step delay shows about 3× gain margin at the actual 100 ms period. In paired trials (n = 8 per condition), the arrival error falls from 4.16 ± 0.94 mm to 0.53 ± 0.21 mm. It stays at 0.5–0.8 mm under odometry drift, cool lighting and intermittent occlusion. Under full occlusion the new pipeline refuses to move, whereas the colour-blob baseline converges with a silent 8.6 mm bias. Taught zero-offset calibration reduces the effect of a 1° camera mounting error from 10.6 mm to 1.04 mm. There were no collisions in 184 trials. About 12,500 *offline* trials show that pure IBVS with a 5 cm marker is nearly unobservable in yaw for this configuration (Jacobian condition number 314).
- **Code-constrained slot planning.** A C++ A\* treats whole straight segments as edges. This lets it enforce building-code rules as hard constraints: no horizontal slots in load-bearing walls, limited horizontal runs, and the conduit bending radius. Optimality is kept with the state (x, y, last direction), which was checked against an independent Dijkstra on 400 random grids. On 140 random walls (*offline*) the planner outputs no violating route, whereas the first-version obstacle-only planner violates the code on 106 walls. For the 106 of 400 walls where no compliant plan was found, the conflict always involves one pair of slots; changing the task instead of the rules (swapping feed points, routing a slot through the floor) gives compliant plans for 102 walls, checked independently (*offline*).
- **Task-level joint planning and execution time.** Station partitioning uses exact dynamic programming, and the visiting order is a closed-form one-dimensional sweep. Station-aware route search moves slot corners onto station-window boundaries, and a lower-bound certificate tests optimality on every wall. On 400 random residential walls (*offline*, model-predicted time), joint planning is never slower than plan-then-partition and is proven optimal on 92–93 % of walls. Its benefit depends on the cost structure of execution. With point-to-point arm motion (a fixed 2 s per waypoint), it saves 3.46 % on non-load-bearing walls (n = 156, 95 % CI [3.02, 3.91] %), and on the room3 example task in Gazebo it took 228 s against 246 s for the rule-based routes (−7.3 %, predicted −6.6 %, 2 + 2 seed-paired runs). With continuous arm trajectories the cost of an extra straight segment drops from 8.1 s to 2.0 s and the predicted saving shrinks to 1.18 % ([1.03, 1.33] %; measured 160 s against 165 s). With the feed limited by the cutting-force model (5.5 mm/s in brick) each waypoint costs 5.1 s, the saving returns to 2.28 % ([1.98, 2.57] %), and the relative gain of station merging falls from about 10 % to 3.3 %. The time model is recalibrated for each execution mode and kept as a named preset; on runs not used for calibration its error in station time stays within 0.7 %. Over 200 random rooms (*offline*), exact subset dynamic programming over wall order and sweep direction is 1.44 % faster than a fixed order ([1.26, 1.64] %) and 0.36 % faster than nearest neighbour; work on the walls is 81 % of the predicted time.
- **Continuous arm trajectories.** Each station's waypoints are executed as one trajectory of jerk-limited S-curve segments, with the feed separated from rapid moves and exact stops at corners. Station time for the joint plan falls from 227.9 s (n = 4) to 160.1 s (n = 3), −29.8 %; the mean tool-path error is unchanged (0.15–0.20 mm against 0.18–0.19 mm). A lateral deviation at the start of vertical cuts was traced to base yaw excited by the preceding repositioning move; a 0.2 s dwell after plunging reduces it from up to 0.93 mm to 0.13–0.15 mm.
- **Multi-station execution and along-wall accuracy.** With ground-truth odometry, two slots (2.76 m, 11 stations) were completed in every end-to-end run. The optimised partition and order reduced station time from 283 s to 250 s (−11.8 %). On the same 11-station plan, a constant per-run lateral offset of +1.26 / +0.65 mm, inherited from the alignment parking residual, fell to +0.24 / −0.18 mm when the EKF-estimated marker became the wall origin (ablation +0.86 mm; +0.65 to +2.29 mm over all five first-version runs). With the encoder error model, a single marker and odometry-only strafing, the along-wall error grew in proportion to the distance from the marker, up to 24.7 mm: a planar wall gives the LiDAR no along-wall information, and the error is a fixed-direction lateral scale error. Re-aligning after a set travel does not help (17.5 mm). A map of AprilTags every 0.6 m combined with online estimation of the lateral odometry scale is now the default: 1.5 mm maximum along-wall error (2 runs per configuration), at a total task time of 295 s against 272 s. Estimating the tag positions as well (EKF-SLAM, *offline*) removes the dependence on how accurately the tags were placed: with 5 mm placement error the mean 11-station maximum error falls from 4.79 mm to 1.16 mm (n = 100).
- **Cutting force and base holding.** A specific-energy cutting-force model and a friction analysis of the locked mecanum wheels show that vertical slots are feasible but horizontal slots at full spindle power need a friction coefficient of about 0.49 (*offline*). After two timing defects were fixed, the offline plant model predicts Gazebo step responses within 6 % in 12 of 14 trials. Cutting a whole wall in brick at 5.5 mm/s, the earlier proportional hold lets the tool tip move 81–85 mm on horizontal slots and covers 40 % of the planned slot, whereas a disturbance observer with 70 % feed-forward gives 5.0–6.2 mm (RMS 1.1–1.2 mm, n = 4) and 100 % coverage, about twice the offline prediction because the force couples with base motion. The feed limit is set by wheel–ground friction, not by spindle power.
- **System layer and execution issues.** Behaviour-tree execution matched the earlier sequential program on the 11-station plan (232.5 s versus 233.2–233.5 s). Over 3 laps of 6 fixed goals, Smac 2D + MPPI reached 18 / 18 goals versus 15 / 18 for NavFn + DWB, kept 183 mm minimum clearance versus 5 mm and used 9.7 % versus 45.5 % of one core in the controller. Fusing the gyro reduced odometry drift over a 129 m route from 1.4–1.6 % to 0.14–0.26 %. Occasional 10–15 s stalls before navigation goals were traced to a 20 ms goal-acknowledgement timeout measured in simulation time while the simulation clock advances in 0.1 s steps; a slow-motion EKF bias was traced to instantaneous twist sampling in the odometry and fixed by averaging (EKF and wheel pose now agree within 0.02 mm).
- **Simulation fidelity.** The base turned at only 18–37 % of the commanded rate. Two interacting causes were found: position-interface arm joints and a planar-move plugin that assigns one velocity to all links. Force-controlled joints and a custom force-based base plugin restore the ratio to 0.99–1.01. A rotation visible only in the base pose, not in link velocities, was removed from the velocity loop and sensors by using pose-differenced velocity.

**Limitations.** All results are from simulation, with idealised rendering, an assumed (not calibrated) encoder error model and a force-driven base whose wheels do not carry the load. Cutting forces come from a model whose specific energy and force ratio are assumed, and the tool never enters the wall. The alignment results and the first multi-station results were obtained with ground-truth odometry. Along-wall accuracy relies on a wall-mounted tag map; online tag-map estimation and room-level planning have only offline results. Every time-model preset was calibrated and checked on one wall with two plans. Samples are small: 8 trials per alignment condition, 2 + 1 runs for the second-version multi-station setup, 2 runs per along-wall configuration, 1–4 runs per cutting-force configuration and 1–3 runs per system-layer configuration.
